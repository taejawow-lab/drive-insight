Low-angle sun is a predictable hazard because it arrives during normal commute windows. The danger is not only brightness; it is the way glare hides brake lights, pedestrians, lane edges, debris, and traffic-signal changes. A good glare plan starts before the first hard-to-see mile: clean glass, usable visor, appropriate sunglasses, slower speed, and a willingness to delay or reroute when the sun lines up with the road.

## Quick decision table

| Situation | Safer choice | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Sun is directly ahead | Lower visor, increase following distance, reduce speed smoothly | Holding speed while squinting |
| Windshield has film or streaks | Clean inside and outside glass before the trip | Wiping glare with a dry sleeve while moving |
| Sunglasses are missing or scratched | Choose an easier route or delay if glare is severe | Driving blind because the trip is routine |
| You cannot see lane edges or brake lights | Pull into a safe legal area and wait for angle or conditions to improve | Stopping abruptly in a travel lane |

## Clean glass changes the whole drive

A windshield can look clean in shade and become a white veil at sunrise or sunset. Interior haze from plastics, dust, fingerprints, and vaping residue scatters light. Exterior film from road spray and wiper wear does the same. Clean both sides before a glare-prone commute, and replace wiper blades when they smear instead of clearing.

Keep the dashboard uncluttered. Papers, glossy objects, and light-colored items can reflect into the windshield. If you use a navigation phone, set it before moving, keep it low and stable, and do not let screen glare compete with the road. The goal is fewer bright surfaces, fewer reflections, and fewer reasons to look away.

## Visor and sunglasses setup

A sun visor is useful only when it is already adjusted and not covered by dangling items. Keep it free of paperwork, badges, and accessories that create shadows or distractions. Polarized sunglasses can reduce reflected glare for many drivers, but they may also interact with some displays. Test them while parked, and make sure you can still read essential instruments without staring.

Do not use very dark lenses at dawn, dusk, or under changing shade if they hide pedestrians and cyclists. The best choice is the one that improves contrast without reducing important detail. If you wear prescription lenses, keep a driving-ready pair in the vehicle or bag rather than relying on fashion sunglasses that do not correct your vision.

## Change following distance before you need it

Glare steals reaction time. Add space early, especially behind motorcycles, bicycles, school buses, delivery vehicles, and cars entering shaded intersections. If the vehicle behind you is tailgating, avoid sudden braking; signal early and let them pass when safe. Your job is to make your own driving predictable even when visibility is imperfect.

At intersections, scan twice. Low sun can make signal heads, turn arrows, and pedestrians hard to distinguish. Slow earlier than usual, check mirrors, and expect other drivers to miss the same cues you are struggling to see. A green light does not mean the crosswalk is clear when glare is hiding movement.

## Route and timing tactics

If a road runs directly east-west, the sun angle can be worst for a short but intense window. Leaving fifteen minutes earlier or later may be safer than fighting the glare. A route with buildings, trees, or a different alignment can reduce exposure even if it adds a minute. For long trips, note sunrise and sunset times and plan breaks around the worst alignment.

Commercial drivers and parents doing school pickup should treat glare as a schedule risk, not an annoyance. Build in enough time that slowing down is an option. The unsafe choice is the route that requires normal speed when normal sight distance is gone.

## If visibility collapses

Do not guess through a bright patch. Ease off the accelerator, keep both hands on the wheel, and avoid sharp steering. If you need to stop, choose a legal parking area, lot, or wide safe turnout; do not stop in a travel lane or just beyond a hill. Turn on appropriate lights when visibility is reduced, but remember that lights do not solve the problem if you cannot see.

After stopping, wait for the sun angle, traffic, or weather to change. Clean the glass if safe. Then re-enter traffic with extra space. A delayed arrival is better than continuing with hidden pedestrians, stopped traffic, or debris ahead.

## FAQ

**Are polarized sunglasses always better?** They often help with reflected glare, but drivers should test visibility of essential displays while parked and avoid lenses that are too dark for changing light.

**Does a clean windshield really matter?** Yes. Film and streaks scatter low-angle light, turning a manageable sun angle into a bright haze.
