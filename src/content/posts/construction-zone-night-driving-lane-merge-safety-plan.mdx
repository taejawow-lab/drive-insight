Night work zones combine several small risks: unfamiliar tapers, temporary pavement markings, glare, workers close to traffic, sudden speed changes, and drivers who notice the merge too late. This guide was checked on 2026-06-10 against FHWA, NHTSA, FMCSA, AAA Foundation, and NOAA resources. It is general safety planning, not legal advice; posted controls, police direction, and current road/weather alerts take priority.

## Decision table

| Situation | Safer choice | Mistake to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Lane taper appears ahead | Ease off and create space early | Racing to the last cone |
| Heavy truck beside you | Avoid lingering in the blind spot | Forcing a merge beside the trailer |
| Glare or rain hides markings | Slow down or reroute | Following taillights blindly |
| Route change needed | Pull over or use passenger help | Changing navigation while moving |

## 1. Reduce speed before the merge feels urgent
The safest work-zone decision happens upstream. Ease off early, increase following distance, and avoid being the driver
who discovers the taper at the last cone. At night, temporary markings can be harder to read, so assume the lane pattern
may change again around the next curve.

## 2. Use zipper logic without forcing it
When traffic is slow and lanes are clearly ending, a calm zipper merge can reduce conflict. That does not mean
accelerating to block someone, diving across solid barriers, or matching speed beside a truck with no escape space. The
useful rule is predictable cooperation, not winning the lane.

## 3. Manage glare and visual overload
Clean glass, adjusted mirrors, and lower cabin brightness help before the trip starts. In rain or fog, cones,
retroreflective barrels, and work lights can bloom into confusing shapes. If you cannot identify lane edges comfortably,
slow down, increase space, or choose a safer route.

## 4. Protect workers and stopped vehicles
Work zones can contain people, equipment, and disabled vehicles where you least expect them. Do not look at your phone
for route changes while moving. Let a passenger navigate or pull over legally before changing plans.

## 5. Decide when the trip is not worth it
If fatigue, weather, detours, or repeated narrow lanes are stacking up, the safest strategy may be delaying or
rerouting. Helpful driving content should normalize lower-risk choices instead of treating every trip as mandatory.
