Severe storms create fast-changing driving risks. This guide gives drivers a warning-stage decision tree for delaying travel, finding shelter, avoiding floodwater, handling hail, and checking the vehicle afterward.

![Severe Thunderstorm Driving Shelter, Hail, and Wind Plan](/images/illustrations/di-thunderstorm-driving-20260612-hero.png)

## Decision table

| Situation | Best next step | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Warning covers your route | Delay or choose a safer shelter plan | Driving into the warned area to save time |
| Road is covered by water | Turn around and choose another route | Testing depth with the vehicle |
| Hail begins while moving | Slow smoothly and seek sturdy cover when safe | Abruptly stopping in a travel lane |
| Storm has passed | Inspect from a safe place before continuing | Ignoring wipers, lights, tires, or glass damage |

![supporting scene](/images/illustrations/di-thunderstorm-driving-20260612-01.png)

## 1. Treat the warning as a route decision
A severe thunderstorm warning is not just background weather. Before leaving, check the current warning polygon, timing,
hail and wind language, and whether a delay is safer than entering the corridor. If you are already driving, look for a
sturdy building, covered parking, or a legal safe pull-off away from trees, power lines, and low spots. Do not stop
under an overpass where traffic and wind hazards can stack up.

![article visual 2](/images/illustrations/di-thunderstorm-driving-20260612-02.png)

## 2. Never trade floodwater for schedule
Water depth and road damage are hard to judge from a driver seat, especially at night or under heavy rain. Turn around
when water covers the road, when barricades are present, or when the path dips under bridges and drainage areas. The
article reinforces the NWS flood-safety message because a short delay is cheaper than a vehicle sweep-away or rescue.

![article visual 3](/images/illustrations/di-thunderstorm-driving-20260612-03.png)

## 3. Handle hail and wind with distance
If hail begins, reduce speed smoothly, increase following distance, and seek sturdy cover only when it can be reached
without sudden lane changes. Damaging wind can move debris, branches, high-profile vehicles, and stopped traffic. Keep
both hands on the wheel, avoid parking under trees, and wait for the core of the storm to pass before inspecting damage.

![article visual 4](/images/illustrations/di-thunderstorm-driving-20260612-04.png)

## 4. Check the vehicle after the storm
After conditions improve, inspect tires, lights, windshield damage, wipers, mirrors, and underbody debris from a safe
location. Photograph damage for records only when you are away from traffic. If glass, tire, steering, braking, or
electrical issues appear, use roadside assistance or professional inspection instead of trying to complete the trip.

![article visual 5](/images/illustrations/di-thunderstorm-driving-20260612-05.png)

## 5. Keep the kit boring and ready
A storm kit should include a charged phone, power bank, flashlight, water, basic first-aid supplies, reflective gear,
and insurance or road-service contacts. The helpful-content goal is not dramatic survival gear; it is a practical habit
that helps readers avoid preventable weather decisions.
